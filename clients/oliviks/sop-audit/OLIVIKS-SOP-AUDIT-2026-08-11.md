## What this is for

An SOP is a written way of doing one job, so the result is the same no matter who is on
shift. How the egusi is portioned. Who watches the Wolt tablet during service. What
happens when an order goes out with an item missing.

Most of that knowledge currently lives in two heads. That works while both of you are
there. It stops working the day you want a holiday, a new hire, a second kitchen, or a
catering job bigger than the two of you can cook.

This audit does not write the SOPs. It finds where they are needed most, so the work goes
to the parts that are costing money and time rather than the parts that are simply
undocumented and fine.

## How to answer

For every statement, pick one:

| Score | Answer |
|---|---|
| 3 | Written down and everyone follows it |
| 2 | Written down somewhere, but not really followed |
| 1 | We do it, but it only lives in our heads |
| 0 | Not happening at all |
| n/a | Does not apply to us |

Answer honestly. "We just know how to do it" is a real answer and a very common one.
Nothing here is a test.

At the end of each area, add three things: who does it today, what goes wrong most often,
and how much the area is hurting the business right now (fine / annoying / costing us).

## Area 1: Opening and closing the kitchen

*The first hour and the last hour of the day.*

1. There is a fixed opening routine, and anyone opening knows exactly what to do first,
   second, third.
2. There is a fixed closing routine, covering the fridges, the gas, the money, the bins
   and locking up.
3. Anyone can tell at a glance whether the kitchen was closed down properly last night.
4. When someone new opens or closes for the first time, they can follow written steps
   instead of being shown.

---

## Area 2: Buying and stock

*Suppliers, orders, storage and waste. Nigerian ingredients in Budapest are not a
supermarket run.*

1. There is a written list of suppliers, what each one supplies, and what we normally pay.
2. There is a set day and a set way of ordering stock, not just ordering when someone
   notices it is finished.
3. There is a par level for key items, so we know the point at which we reorder.
   (Par level means the minimum amount that should always be on the shelf.)
4. Deliveries are checked in against the order, and short or wrong deliveries are recorded.
5. We know how much food we throw away in a normal week, and why.

---

## Area 3: Cooking and portion consistency

*Whether a customer gets the same plate on a Tuesday from Cynthia and on a Saturday from
someone else.*

1. Every dish on the menu has a written recipe with quantities, not just a method someone
   knows.
2. Batch sizes are set. We know how much egusi one pot makes and how many portions come
   out of it.
3. Portioning is standardised, with a set scoop, ladle or container per dish. (This is
   usually the single biggest hidden cost in a kitchen.)
4. Someone tastes and checks the food before service, against a known standard.
5. A new cook could produce the food to your standard from the written recipes, without
   you correcting them.

---

## Area 4: Food safety and hygiene

*What keeps you legal in Hungary and what keeps customers safe.*

1. HACCP paperwork is current and someone is clearly responsible for keeping it up to date.
2. Fridge and freezer temperatures are recorded on a schedule, not only when someone
   remembers.
3. There is a written cleaning schedule saying what gets cleaned daily, weekly and
   monthly, and by whom.
4. Allergens per dish are written down, and staff can answer an allergy question correctly
   without asking you. (Peanut, gluten and fish come up constantly on Nigerian menus.)
5. Cooked food is labelled and dated, and there is a clear rule on how long anything is kept.
6. If NÉBIH walked in unannounced tomorrow morning, the paperwork would be ready.

---

## Area 5: Taking orders

*You take orders through at least six different doors. Each one is a chance to lose one.*

1. Every order channel has one named person responsible for watching it during service.
   (Walk in, phone, WhatsApp, shop.oliviks.com, Wolt, Foodora, catering enquiries.)
2. All orders end up in one place the kitchen looks at, rather than being spread across a
   tablet, a phone and a notebook.
3. There is a rule for how fast a WhatsApp, phone or email enquiry must be answered, and
   someone checks it is being met.
4. When we are sold out of a dish, there is a set routine for switching it off everywhere.
   (Website, shop, Wolt and Foodora all need updating, not just one.)
5. Order information taken by phone or WhatsApp is written down the same way every time,
   so nothing is missed.

---

## Area 6: Packing and handing over

*From the pot to the customer's hand. Where most complaints are actually born.*

1. There is a set way of packing each type of order, including which container, how soup
   and swallow are separated, and what goes in the bag.
2. Every order is checked against the ticket before it leaves the kitchen, by someone
   other than the person who packed it.
3. There is a clear routine for handing an order to a Wolt or Foodora courier, so the
   right bag goes to the right rider.
4. Pickup customers are handled the same way every time, including how they are told the
   order is ready.
5. When an order goes out wrong or incomplete, there is a set response and someone records it.

---

## Area 7: Menu and prices across channels

*The same dish currently carries three different prices. In store, on your own shop, and
on Wolt. That is normal, but it has to be managed on purpose.*

1. There is one master menu list that every other place copies from. (The menu currently
   appears on the website, the shop, Wolt, Foodora, Google and the printed menu.)
2. There is a written rule for how the online price is worked out from the in store price.
3. When a price changes, there is a set list of every place that must be updated, and
   someone owns that list.
4. Dish names and spellings are the same everywhere. (For example okro against okra, or
   the same soup listed under two different names.)
5. Someone checks, on a schedule, that Wolt and Foodora still match the real menu and the
   real address.

---

## Area 8: Money and admin

*Cash, card, invoices, tax. The part that is easiest to leave until it is a problem.*

1. There is a daily routine for counting and recording takings, and it is done at the same
   time every day.
2. Cash and card takings are reconciled against orders, so a difference would be noticed.
3. Invoices are issued in a set way, and we always know which ones are unpaid.
4. Supplier bills and business costs are recorded as they happen, not gathered up at the
   end of the month.
5. We know what a normal week costs us to run, and roughly what each dish costs to make.
6. Someone other than the owner could do the daily money routine correctly from written steps.

---

## Area 9: Catering and events

*The highest value orders you take, and usually the least standardised.*

1. There is a set way of handling a catering enquiry, from first message to quote.
2. Catering prices are worked out from a written formula, not judged case by case.
3. There is a deposit and cancellation rule, and it is told to the customer in writing
   before we start.
4. There is a checklist for the event itself, covering transport, serving equipment,
   timings and who is on site.
5. After an event, there is a set follow up, so a good catering customer becomes a repeat one.

---

## Area 10: Customers, complaints and reviews

*You hold a 4.8 rating from over 160 reviews. That is an asset with no written rules
protecting it.*

1. There is a set way of handling an unhappy customer in the shop, and staff know how far
   they can go without asking you.
2. There is a written rule for refunds, replacements and free items.
3. Every Google review gets a reply, and there is a routine for who does it and how fast.
4. Complaints are recorded somewhere, so a repeating problem would be noticed.
5. There is a set way of asking a happy customer for a review.

---

## Area 11: Marketing routines

*The systems built during the Foundation only pay off if they are run on a schedule.*

1. The weekly specials email goes out on a fixed day, by a named person.
2. The WhatsApp broadcast goes out on a fixed day, by a named person.
3. Posts and photos go up on Google Business Profile on a schedule, not only when someone
   remembers.
4. Social media posting has a set rhythm and someone owns it.
5. Photos of new dishes are taken and stored in one place, ready to use.
6. Someone looks at the numbers monthly and can say whether any of this is working.

---

## Area 12: People and training

*Everything above only holds if the people can carry it.*

1. There is a written description of what each role does, even if the roles are family.
2. A new person is trained against a written plan, rather than by watching.
3. Shifts are planned in advance and everyone knows where to look for them.
4. There is a set way of handling a sick day or a no show.
5. Pay, hours and any agreements are recorded in writing.

---

## Area 13: Accounts, access and keys

*Who can get into what. This is the one that hurts most when a phone is lost or a person
leaves.*

1. There is one list of every account the business uses, and who can log into each one.
   (Website, shop admin, MailerLite, Supabase, Namecheap, Google Business Profile, Wolt,
   Foodora, the bank, WhatsApp Business.)
2. Passwords are stored somewhere safe and shared, not only in one person's head or phone.
3. At least two people can get into the critical accounts.
4. If the business phone was lost today, we know exactly what we would lose access to and
   what we would do.
5. Keys to the premises are accounted for, and we know who holds one.

---

## Last part: the honest questions

1. If you were away for two weeks with no phone, what breaks first? Be specific. Not
   "everything". What actually goes wrong on day one or day two?
2. What can only you do, that nobody else in the business can do?
3. What question do your staff or family ask you over and over again? Whatever you answer
   more than twice a week is a missing SOP.
4. If we could only standardise three areas this year, which three?
5. What would need to be true in twelve months for you to say this was worth doing?
6. Anything else we should know.

---

## Scoring

Per area: add the scores, ignore anything marked n/a, divide by (3 × number answered),
turn into a percentage.

| Overall | What it means |
|---|---|
| Under 25% | Nothing to hand over yet. Almost everything runs on the people in the room. |
| 25 to 49% | Runs on people, not on paper. The business works because you carry it. That is also the ceiling. |
| 50 to 74% | Half written, half remembered. The bones exist. Consistency and ownership are missing. |
| 75% and above | Mostly systemised. The work is tightening weak areas and keeping documents current. |

Priority is not simply the lowest score. It is the lowest score in an area you also marked
as costing money, time or customers.
